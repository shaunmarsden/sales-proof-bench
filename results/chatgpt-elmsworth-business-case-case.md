### 1. Executive Summary

Elmsworth Retail recently completed a three-week pilot in which four customer support agents used an AI drafting tool to help prepare customer email replies. The pilot produced an encouraging qualitative signal. I observed that the participating agents appeared to be progressing through emails more quickly, and some of the participants described the tool as a time-saver. However, the pilot did not collect the operational or financial data required to quantify the impact. No time-tracking data was collected, and no ticket-volume, first-response-time or resolution-time metrics were analysed. It would therefore be inappropriate to claim a percentage improvement, a specific number of hours saved, a financial return or an improvement in customer satisfaction. I am requesting approval for a measured rollout across the 20-agent support team, implemented with a defined baseline, consistent performance measures and a formal review point. The purpose of the rollout would be both to provide access to the tool and to establish whether the positive early feedback translates into measurable operational and financial value.

### 2. Decision Requested

Approve budget for a controlled rollout of the AI drafting tool across the 20-agent Customer Support team, subject to: establishing baseline performance before full implementation; defining the operational and quality measures that will be monitored; recording the complete cost of the tool and its implementation; introducing the tool in a way that allows meaningful comparison; and reviewing the evidence before making any long-term return-on-investment claim.

### 3. Confirmed Information

The pilot lasted three weeks. Four support agents participated. The agents used an AI drafting tool for customer email replies. Marcus observed that the team appeared to be progressing through emails more quickly. An unspecified number of the four participating agents said that the tool felt like a time-saver. No time-tracking data was collected before or during the pilot. No ticket-volume data was pulled. No first-response-time data was pulled. No resolution-time data was pulled. Marcus would like budget to roll the tool out to all 20 support agents.

### 4. Direct Evidence From the Pilot

Marcus's direct statement following the pilot was: "Honestly the team seems to be getting through emails quicker, a few of them mentioned it feels like a real time-saver." This statement provides limited qualitative evidence that Marcus perceived that the pilot participants were progressing through emails more quickly, and an unspecified subset of the four participants felt that the tool saved them time. It does not establish how many agents experienced a benefit; how much time was saved; whether the observation applied consistently throughout the pilot; whether the full team would experience the same result; or whether any operational or customer outcome changed.

### 5. Business Rationale

The pilot provides an initial reason to investigate the tool further. The combination of Marcus's observation and positive informal feedback from some pilot participants suggests that AI-assisted drafting may have the potential to support more efficient email handling. The appropriate business case is therefore not that a financial return has already been demonstrated. The case is that the pilot produced a sufficiently positive early signal to justify a controlled, measurable rollout. A wider measured rollout would allow Elmsworth Retail to determine whether agents spend less time drafting suitable customer replies; any time released translates into greater support capacity; first-response or resolution performance changes; the quality and accuracy of customer communications are maintained; the experience is consistent across the wider team; and any verified operational benefit is sufficient to justify the total cost.

### 6. Assumptions and Hypotheses

The following are reasonable hypotheses to test, but they are not confirmed findings: AI-assisted drafting may reduce the time required to prepare some customer email replies. Benefits experienced by some pilot participants may also be experienced by other agents. Faster drafting may contribute to increased handling capacity or improved response performance. The tool may be useful across a broader range of customer enquiries than those encountered during the pilot. Any verified benefit may be sufficient to justify the cost of a wider rollout. These points must remain clearly labelled as assumptions until supported by measured evidence.

### 7. Evidence Gaps for a Credible Return-on-Investment Claim

A credible return-on-investment claim would require evidence in the following areas.

**A. Complete cost of the rollout:** licences for the intended users; implementation and configuration; integration, where applicable; training and onboarding; ongoing administration and support; and any additional quality, security or management activity required. Without the complete cost, it is not possible to calculate the investment against which any return would be assessed.

**B. Pre-rollout operational baseline:** customer email or ticket volume; active time spent preparing and reviewing email replies; first-response time; resolution time; reopen, escalation or rework rates; quality-review results; and customer satisfaction measures, where available. These measures would need clear definitions so that the same activity is measured before and after implementation.

**C. Post-rollout results:** the same measures collected after implementation, showing whether performance changed; the size and consistency of any change; whether changes were experienced across the wider team or only by particular agents; whether outcomes varied by enquiry type or complexity; and whether improvements were sustained rather than temporary.

**D. Tool usage and adoption:** which agents used the tool; how frequently it was used; the types of customer emails for which it was used; whether drafted responses required substantial editing; and whether agents who used the tool more consistently experienced different outcomes from those who used it less frequently. Without adoption data, any change in performance could not be reliably connected to the tool.

**E. Quality and customer outcomes:** accuracy; tone and suitability; compliance with support standards; rework or correction; escalations or complaints; and customer satisfaction, where measured. The existing pilot provides no evidence that customer satisfaction improved, declined or remained unchanged.

**F. Attribution:** a reasonable way to distinguish the effect of the tool from other factors, including changes in ticket demand; differences in case complexity; staffing changes; seasonal effects; process changes; and differences between individual agents. A phased introduction or another consistent comparison method would make attribution more credible than simply comparing two periods with no controls.

**G. Conversion of operational benefits into financial value:** the business would need evidence that released capacity resulted in an identifiable economic outcome, such as avoided additional staffing requirements; additional customer demand being handled; reduced backlog; reduced overtime; or staff capacity being redirected to other valuable work. Time that appears to have been saved should not automatically be described as cash savings. The valuation method would need to be agreed using Elmsworth Retail's actual cost and operating data.

### 8. What the Case Says Without That Evidence

In the absence of the required baseline, operational and financial data, this case does not claim a quantified return on investment. Instead, it states: a three-week pilot involving four support agents produced an encouraging but limited qualitative signal. Marcus observed that the participants appeared to progress through emails more quickly, and an unspecified number of the participants described the tool as a time-saver. The pilot was not designed or measured in a way that allows the impact to be quantified. A controlled rollout with baseline and post-implementation measurement is recommended to determine whether the perceived benefit translates into measurable operational and financial value. This is a case for measured validation, not a claim of proven savings.

### 9. Recommended Measurement Path

**Step 1, agree the decision criteria:** before the wider rollout begins, Marcus and the VP should agree which operational outcomes matter; which quality measures must be protected; what costs will be included; what would count as a successful result; and whether the purpose is increased capacity, improved response performance, avoided cost or another defined outcome.

**Step 2, establish a real baseline:** collect current performance data using the existing support process before agents begin using the tool more broadly, using consistent definitions across the agreed operational, quality and customer measures. Where drafting time is important, it should be measured directly through an agreed method rather than estimated retrospectively. Structured feedback should also be collected from the participating agents, distinguishing individual experiences rather than treating comments from "a few" agents as representative of the entire team.

**Step 3, use a phased rollout:** introduce the tool in phases rather than switching every agent at the same time, allowing comparison of agents using the tool with agents still using the existing process, each participating agent's performance against their own baseline, and performance across different types of customer enquiry. The rollout should also record actual use of the tool so that results can be assessed against adoption.

**Step 4, monitor quality alongside efficiency:** any evidence of faster drafting or increased capacity should be reviewed together with quality results. The tool should not be considered successful solely because agents appear to work more quickly.

**Step 5, collect representative feedback:** use structured feedback from all participating agents rather than relying solely on informal comments from a subset, covering where the tool saves time; where it does not help; how much editing drafts require; which enquiry types are suitable; any quality or accuracy concerns; and barriers to consistent use.

**Step 6, calculate return only after the evidence exists:** once actual costs, baseline results and post-rollout results are available, assess whether a measurable operational improvement occurred; whether it can reasonably be attributed to the tool; whether it produced an identifiable business benefit; and whether that benefit exceeded the complete cost of implementation. No return-on-investment percentage, payback period or financial saving should be presented before those inputs are available.

### 10. What Must Not Be Presented as Confirmed

The tool saved a specific percentage of agent time. The tool saved a specific number of hours. The tool produced a specific financial saving or return. All four pilot agents experienced a time-saving benefit. The phrase "a few" means a particular number of agents. The experience of the agents who commented represents the experience of the full pilot group. The experience of four pilot agents represents the likely experience of all 20 agents. Ticket volume increased. First-response time improved. Resolution time improved. Customer satisfaction improved. Response quality was maintained or improved. The tool caused agents to progress through emails more quickly. Any perceived time saving translated into reduced costs or additional capacity. The pilot demonstrated a positive return on investment. Marcus's VP requires a quantified return-on-investment case. Marcus's VP would approve a qualitative case without financial evidence.

### 11. Recommendation

Approve a controlled, measured rollout rather than presenting the pilot as proof of financial return. The initial pilot offers a positive qualitative reason to continue testing, but it does not provide enough evidence to quantify the impact. Establishing a real baseline and collecting consistent results during the rollout will allow Elmsworth Retail to make a later decision based on measured operational performance, quality outcomes and actual cost. This approach allows the business to act on the encouraging early feedback without overstating what the pilot demonstrated.
